# How much does boosting improve NYC home price predictions?

## Group
The Outliers

## Topic
Own: a comparison of ridge regression and gradient boosting for residential property sales.

## Question
How much does gradient boosting improve future sale-price predictions over a well-specified ridge model for NYC one- to three-family homes?

## Why it matters
Buyers, sellers, and housing analysts need to know whether a more complex model provides a meaningful accuracy gain over a simpler benchmark. Measuring the improvement on later transactions, overall and by borough, will show where added complexity is useful. The goal is prediction, not causal explanation.

## Data
NYC Department of Finance: https://www.nyc.gov/site/finance/property/property-rolling-sales-data.page
We loaded all five borough workbooks: 82,576 records, October 1, 2025-September 30, 2026, with 21 columns: SALE PRICE, empty EASEMENT, and 19 other fields. Categories 01, 02, and 03 identify 36,888 one-, two-, and three-family dwelling sales, including 17,196 nonpositive-price records. A pre-specified $10,000 minimum screens nominal transfers, leaving 18,672 observations and no exact duplicate rows: 14,227 before July 2026 and 4,445 afterward. 

## Baseline
Ridge regression of natural-log sale price, selecting its penalty from 12 pre-specified values. Use 11 source fields: borough, neighborhood, ZIP code, building category, building class at sale, residential and commercial unit counts, land and gross areas, construction year, and sale date. Engineer log areas, property age and age squared, elapsed time, and borough-by-log-area interactions. One-hot encode categorical fields. Treat nonpositive areas and invalid construction years as missing; add missingness indicators. Fit median imputation, rare-category grouping, encoding, and numeric scaling inside each training fold. Exclude addresses, parcel identifiers, and classifications labeled "at present."

## Comparison
XGBoost on the same log target and engineered inputs, with 12 pre-specified configurations of depth, learning rate, and regularization and a fixed tree budget. This develops the notebook's main nonlinear method while giving ridge a stronger specification. Omit stacking to keep the comparison focused.

## Evaluation
Hold July-September 2026 out until all choices are fixed. Use expanding-window validation: train October-December, validate January-February; train through February, validate March-April; train through April, validate May-June. Tune both models by mean absolute log-price error, then refit through June. Report test log-price MAE, its percentage reduction versus ridge, and dollar MAE after exponentiating predictions. Estimate 95% intervals with 1,000 paired bootstrap resamples of tax blocks within boroughs, keeping model predictions paired. These intervals describe sampling uncertainty within the observed quarter, not uncertainty across future market conditions. Report borough results as secondary analyses and intervals containing zero as inconclusive.

## Key figure
Horizontal axis: all NYC and each borough. Vertical axis: percentage reduction in log-price MAE relative to ridge. Points and 95% paired-bootstrap intervals show XGBoost's improvement; zero marks equal accuracy. Label sample sizes.

## Risks and fallback
Check price-floor sensitivity at $1,000 and $50,000 without retuning on test results. Sparse Manhattan observations may make its comparison inconclusive. Snapshot attributes may reflect later updates, limiting claims about real-time deployment. If tuning is costly, reduce the grid before test evaluation. If gains are small, document where ridge suffices rather than add models.
